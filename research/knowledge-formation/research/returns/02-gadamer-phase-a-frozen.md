## Phase-A repository-blind literature map

### Bottom-line finding

“Knowledge formation” is coherent in this corpus only as a provisional comparative heading. It is not a shared native object, natural kind, or universal sequence.

The surveyed traditions study at least nine different phenomena:

1. an event of interpretive understanding;
2. the historical-social conditioning of intelligibility;
3. entry into competent practice;
4. the stabilization and warranting of facts;
5. cognition distributed across people and artifacts;
6. testimonial or institutional dependence;
7. preservation and forgetting through infrastructures;
8. attribution of authority and credit;
9. the production of ignorance, unintelligibility, and silence.

These cannot safely be collapsed into one pipeline. A defensible comparison must specify:

\[
\langle\text{epistemic achievement},\ \text{bearer},\ \text{practice/media},\ \text{warrant or authority regime},\ \text{temporal-material supports}\rangle.
\]

Without those indices, “knowledge forms” equivocates between understanding, learning, belief acquisition, fact construction, certification, memory, and recognition as a knower.

### Citation chains followed

- **Hermeneutics:** Dilthey and Heidegger → Gadamer → Habermas’s ideology-critique objection, Ricoeur’s attempted mediation, and the Gadamer–Derrida dispute over alterity and understanding. The authoritative hermeneutics overview confirms Gadamer’s dependence on effective history and the Habermasian concern that apparent agreement may be ideologically distorted ([SEP, “Hermeneutics”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2022/entries/hermeneutics/)).
- **Sociology/STS:** Mannheim → Fleck → Kuhn → Bloor, Latour and Woolgar, Knorr Cetina, and Longino. Later counterpositions were pursued through Hacking, Goldman, Boghossian, and Latour’s own 2004 reconsideration.
- **Practice/distribution:** apprenticeship and situated activity → Lave and Wenger → Hutchins’s cultural cognitive systems → Giere and Moffatt’s merger of cognitive and social analysis.
- **Anthropological comparison:** Evans-Pritchard/Winch rationality problem → Hollis and Lukes → Verran and Viveiros de Castro; the Sahlins–Obeyesekere controversy was included as a concrete dispute about voice, rationality, and ethnographic attribution.
- **Memory/infrastructure:** Halbwachs → Star/Bowker classification and boundary objects → Bowker on scientific memory and Taylor on embodied repertoire.
- **Power and exclusion:** Harding/standpoint traditions → Fricker’s epistemic injustice → Dotson’s silencing and epistemic violence; Smith, Wilson, TallBear, and de la Cadena supplied differently located Indigenous and decolonial challenges.

### Inclusion and exclusion rules

Included:

- primary theoretical works that explicitly alter the bearer, production, warrant, transmission, or persistence of knowledge;
- ethnographic studies showing mechanisms in practice;
- primary counterpositions defending truth, realism, or universal rational appraisal;
- authoritative reference works used to disambiguate debates;
- stable publisher, journal, DOI, or library records.

Excluded:

- generic “knowledge management,” organizational-learning, and DIKW literature when it presupposed a data→information→knowledge progression without defending it;
- machine-learning “knowledge formation” literature, except where technology bears directly on testimony or distribution;
- cognitive-developmental and animal social-learning literatures, because they would constitute distinct domains rather than evidence about the present social-comparative corpus;
- popular summaries and unsourced web taxonomies;
- theological accounts of revelation and large portions of legal epistemology;
- broad claims about “Asian,” “African,” or “Indigenous” epistemology unsupported by community- and practice-specific work.

The exclusions are substantive limits, not claims of irrelevance.

### Stopping rationale

Searching stopped at conceptual saturation rather than bibliographic exhaustiveness. Each further cluster reproduced one of the established fault lines—individual/system bearer, discovery/construction, truth/authorization, archive/repertoire, translation/equivocation, or understanding/ideology—while adding metadata rather than a new mechanism. This is a structured problem map, not a systematic review, and it does not establish corpus closure.

## Native problem map

### 1. Understanding as an event, not an acquired epistemic object

Gadamer’s primary object is *Verstehen*: understanding that happens through historically situated encounter. Prejudices are enabling anticipations as well as possible errors; tradition is not merely a stock of propositions; and application is internal to understanding. A “fusion of horizons” changes the context within which something can show up as intelligible. It is therefore misleading to model understanding as content manufactured and deposited in an unchanged subject.

Owner: Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method*, originally 1960; revised English translation by Joel Weinsheimer and Donald G. Marshall (London/New York: Continuum/Bloomsbury, 2004/2013). [Publisher record](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/truth-and-method-9781780936246/).

The important negative result is that there is no pre-interpretive starting point. Understanding always begins inside an effective history. Change occurs when anticipations are put at risk by the matter or interlocutor, not by executing a universal production method. The authoritative Gadamer account describes understanding as forming a new context in which familiar and alien are both altered ([SEP, “Hans-Georg Gadamer”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2021/entries/gadamer/)).

Counterposition: Habermas objects that tradition-mediated agreement may reproduce domination. Hermeneutic openness alone may not identify systematically distorted communication. The strongest version of the objection is that Gadamer gives insufficient independent grounds for distinguishing truth disclosed in dialogue from ideology reproduced through dialogue ([SEP, “Hermeneutics”](https://plato.sydney.edu.au/entries/hermeneutics)).

### 2. Social location configures intelligibility

Mannheim treats modes of thought as related to social and historical position rather than as free-floating products of isolated minds. Ideology can stabilize an order; utopian thought can orient transformative action. The difficulty is reflexivity: if all thought is socially located, the sociology of knowledge is located too.

Owner: Karl Mannheim, *Ideology and Utopia: An Introduction to the Sociology of Knowledge*, trans. Louis Wirth and Edward Shils (London: Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1936; original German 1929). [Stable edition record](https://www.routledge.com/Ideology-and-Utopia/Mannheim/p/book/9781315002828).

Harding transforms location from a source of distortion alone into a possible critical resource. Marginalized standpoints are not automatically possessed by identity; they are achievements cultivated through critical-political inquiry.

Owner: Sandra Harding, *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge? Thinking from Women’s Lives* (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1991), ISBN 9780801425134. [Publisher record](https://www.cornellpress.cornell.edu/book/9780801425134/whose-science-whose-knowledge/).

Disagreement: social situation can be treated as causal determination, interpretive horizon, source of epistemic advantage, or target of corrective institutional design. These are not interchangeable meanings of “situated knowledge.”

### 3. Facts stabilize within historically organized collectives

For Fleck, a scientific fact develops inside a *thought collective* and *thought style*. It is neither a simple private invention nor an unmediated object waiting to be read off nature. Historical inheritance shapes what counts as a problem, observation, and resistance.

Owner: Ludwik Fleck, *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact*, ed. Thaddeus J. Trenn and Robert K. Merton, trans. Fred Bradley and Thaddeus J. Trenn (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1979/1981; German original 1935), ISBN 9780226253251. [Publisher record](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/G/bo25676016.html).

Kuhn relocates scientific change to paradigm-governed communities: normal science extends exemplars until anomalies, crisis, and reorganization alter the field’s problems and standards. This is episodic reconfiguration, not monotonic accumulation.

Owner: Thomas S. Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 4th ed. (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2012; original 1962). [Publisher record](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo13179781.html).

Bloor’s strong programme demands causal, impartial, symmetrical, and reflexive explanation of accepted and rejected beliefs. It thereby makes the sociology of knowledge accountable for truths as well as errors.

Owner: David Bloor, *Knowledge and Social Imagery*, 2nd ed. (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1991; original 1976). [Publisher record](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/K/bo3684600.html).

Counterpositions:

- Hacking asks “the social construction of what?” and distinguishes constructions of classifications, people, ideas, objects, and facts rather than accepting construction as a unitary claim: Ian Hacking, *The Social Construction of What?* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1999/2000). [Stable book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Social_Construction_of_What.html?id=C3ILEAAAQBAJ).
- Boghossian rejects strong constructivist and relativist accounts of facts and justification: Paul Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2006), DOI [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199287185.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199287185.001.0001).
- Latour later warned that indiscriminate debunking of facts could aid denialism and proposed “matters of concern”: Bruno Latour, “Why Has Critique Run out of Steam?”, *Critical Inquiry* 30.2 (2004): 225–248, DOI [10.1086/421123](https://doi.org/10.1086/421123).

Thus “socially made” cannot itself decide whether a claim is true, well warranted, imposed, robust, or fragile.

### 4. Knowing as competent participation

Lave and Wenger shift the unit from internal acquisition to changing participation in a community of practice. Newcomers become knowledgeable through legitimate peripheral participation, access to activities and artifacts, identity transformation, and movement toward fuller participation.

Owners: Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, *Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1991), DOI [10.1017/CBO9780511815355](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355).

This model explains embodied and tacit competence that proposition-centered epistemology misses. It also reveals a failure mechanism: exclusion from legitimate participation can prevent learning regardless of an individual’s cognitive capacity.

Limit: not every community reproduces itself through peripheral-to-central movement; some guard secrecy, distribute complementary roles, prevent full membership, or depend on rupture rather than apprenticeship.

### 5. Cognition can belong to an organized system

Hutchins’s ship-navigation ethnography treats the navigation team, instruments, representational media, procedures, and environment as a cognitive system with properties not reducible to those of any one sailor.

Owner: Edwin Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1995/1996), ISBN 9780262581462. [Publisher record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262581462/cognition-in-the-wild/).

Giere and Moffatt explicitly use distributed cognition to question the boundary between cognitive and social explanation: Ronald N. Giere and Barton Moffatt, “Distributed Cognition: Where the Cognitive and the Social Merge,” *Social Studies of Science* 33.2 (2003): 301–310, DOI [10.1177/03063127030332017](https://doi.org/10.1177/03063127030332017).

This creates a bearer problem:

- Is the knower the individual who can answer?
- the team that reliably performs?
- the institution retaining procedures?
- or the coupled human-artifact system?

No neutral aggregation rule settles these cases.

### 6. Epistemic dependence and critical organization

Hardwig argues that advanced knowledge frequently depends on evidence no individual possesses. Rationality can require dependence on experts; some scientific knowledge may be known by a community without being fully known by any member.

Owner: John Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” *Journal of Philosophy* 82.7 (1985): 335–349, DOI [10.2307/2026523](https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523).

Goldman’s veritistic social epistemology evaluates social practices by their tendency to produce true rather than false belief. It resists reducing epistemic success to consensus or social acceptance.

Owner: Alvin I. Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1999), ISBN 9780198238201/9780191519284. [Stable record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Knowledge_in_a_Social_World.html?id=lQdREAAAQBAJ).

Longino’s critical contextual empiricism instead makes objectivity an achievement of appropriately organized criticism: venues for criticism, uptake, shared standards, and tempered equality of intellectual authority.

Owner: Helen E. Longino, *Science as Social Knowledge* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 1990) and *The Fate of Knowledge* (Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2002). Her later formulation is identified as “critical contextual empiricism” in the scholarly discussion linked at [Studies in History and Philosophy of Science](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S003936810400130X).

The disagreement is not simply “individual versus society.” It concerns whether social relations merely transmit warrant, generate it, distribute it, or constitute the standards under which it exists.

### 7. Epistemic cultures and heterogeneous cooperation

Knorr Cetina’s comparative ethnography of high-energy physics and molecular biology shows that sciences differ in their objects, instruments, empirical arrangements, self-relations, and warranting machinery. “Science” does not have one knowledge-making culture.

Owner: Karin Knorr Cetina, *Epistemic Cultures: How the Sciences Make Knowledge* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1999), DOI [10.4159/9780674039681](https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674039681).

Star and Griesemer explain cooperation without consensus through boundary objects: artifacts sufficiently adaptable to local worlds yet stable enough to coordinate across them.

Owners: Susan Leigh Star and James R. Griesemer, “Institutional Ecology, ‘Translations’ and Boundary Objects,” *Social Studies of Science* 19.3 (1989): 387–420, DOI [10.1177/030631289019003001](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631289019003001).

This is crucial against a Gadamerian overextension: practical coordination need not require a fusion into shared meaning. Persistent difference may be a condition of cooperation, not a residue awaiting resolution.

### 8. Infrastructures preserve by classifying and forgetting

Bowker and Star show classifications and standards as durable social-material arrangements. They make some entities visible and actionable while rendering others residual, illegible, or impossible.

Owners: Geoffrey C. Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, *Sorting Things Out: Classification and Its Consequences* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1999), ISBN 9780262024617. [Publisher record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262024617/sorting-things-out/).

Bowker shows that scientific memory depends on contemporary recording technologies and conventions; preservation always entails selective forgetting.

Owner: Geoffrey C. Bowker, *Memory Practices in the Sciences* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 2005/2006), ISBN 9780262025898. [Publisher record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262025898/memory-practices-in-the-sciences/).

Halbwachs identifies collective frameworks through which groups reconstruct memory rather than simply retrieve a stored past.

Owner: Maurice Halbwachs, *On Collective Memory*, ed. and trans. Lewis A. Coser (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1992; drawing on works of 1925 and 1941), ISBN 9780226115962. [Publisher record](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/O/bo3619875.html).

Taylor refuses to identify persistence with archival inscription. Embodied repertoire—gesture, movement, speech, dance, performance—also stores and transmits knowledge.

Owner: Diana Taylor, *The Archive and the Repertoire* (Durham: Duke University Press, 2003). [Publisher record](https://www.dukeupress.edu/the-archive-and-the-repertoire).

Archive and repertoire resist unification: one is not an immature or informal version of the other.

### 9. Attribution is socially allocated and may diverge from contribution

Knowledge becoming attributable is not identical to knowledge arising. Scientific credit is shaped by prestige, priority conventions, authorship, institutional visibility, and cumulative advantage. Merton’s Matthew effect predicts that already eminent actors receive disproportionate recognition.

Owner: Robert K. Merton, “The Matthew Effect in Science,” *Science* 159.3810 (1968): 56–63, DOI [10.1126/science.159.3810.56](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56).

Boundary objects and distributed cognition intensify the problem: a result may depend on technicians, samples, software, classifications, communities, and instruments while credit remains individualized. Attribution is therefore an institutional output, not a transparent readout of causal contribution.

### 10. Knowledge can fail through organized ignorance and failed uptake

Fricker distinguishes testimonial injustice—credibility deficit due to identity prejudice—from hermeneutical injustice, where unequal participation in collective meaning-making leaves experience difficult to understand or communicate.

Owner: Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice: Power and the Ethics of Knowing* (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2007), DOI [10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001).

Dotson locates epistemic violence in hearers’ failure, owing to pernicious ignorance, to meet speakers’ vulnerabilities.

Owner: Kristie Dotson, “Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing,” *Hypatia* 26.2 (2011): 236–257, DOI [10.1111/j.1527-2001.2011.01177.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1527-2001.2011.01177.x).

Mills and later epistemologies-of-ignorance work show ignorance as actively produced and maintained, not merely an empty absence: Shannon Sullivan and Nancy Tuana, eds., *Race and Epistemologies of Ignorance* (Albany: SUNY Press, 2007), ISBN 9780791471029. [Publisher record](https://sunypress.edu/Books/R/Race-and-Epistemologies-of-Ignorance).

Accordingly, failure occurs at multiple points:

- exclusion from participation;
- credibility suppression;
- absence of shared interpretive resources;
- incompatible classification;
- infrastructural erasure;
- secrecy or dispossession;
- successful production of falsehood or ignorance;
- failure to attribute knowledge to its actual holders.

## Comparative and Indigenous limits

Barth offers a useful but limited comparative heuristic: traditions of knowledge have at least a substantive corpus, media of representation, and social organization, with tradition-specific relations among them.

Owner: Fredrik Barth, “An Anthropology of Knowledge,” *Current Anthropology* 43.1 (2002): 1–18, DOI [10.1086/324131](https://doi.org/10.1086/324131).

This is preferable to defining every tradition by propositions alone, but even the three-face model may over-unify cases in which knowing cannot be detached from being, doing, relation, land, or obligation.

Specific comparative correctives include:

- Helen Verran, *Science and an African Logic* (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2001), ISBN 9780226853895. Her Yoruba classroom ethnography treats numbers and certainty as enacted in different social-material practices, not as culturally colored versions of one already-fixed abstraction. [Publisher record](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo3631540.html).
- Linda Tuhiwai Smith, *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples*, 3rd ed. (London: Zed/Bloomsbury, 2021; original 1999), ISBN 9781350346086. Research itself is examined as an imperial regime of extraction and authorization. [Publisher record](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/decolonizing-methodologies-9781350346086/).
- Shawn Wilson, *Research Is Ceremony: Indigenous Research Methods* (Black Point, NS: Fernwood, 2008), ISBN 9781552662816. Relational accountability joins ontology, epistemology, methodology, and axiology rather than treating knowledge as detachable content. [Publisher record](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/research-is-ceremony/9781552662816/).
- Kim TallBear, *Native American DNA* (Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press, 2013), DOI [10.5749/minnesota/9780816665853.001.0001](https://doi.org/10.5749/minnesota/9780816665853.001.0001). Genetic objects and claims of belonging are forged through laboratories, markets, governance, kinship, and tribal authority; biological evidence does not unilaterally determine political belonging.
- Marisol de la Cadena, *Earth Beings* (Durham: Duke University Press, 2015), DOI [10.1215/9780822375265](https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822375265). “Partial connections” allow collaboration while resisting reduction of earth-beings to cultural beliefs about a universally given nature.
- Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, “Perspectival Anthropology and the Method of Controlled Equivocation,” *Tipití* 2.1 (2004), DOI [10.70845/2572-3626.1010](https://doi.org/10.70845/2572-3626.1010). Comparison should preserve equivocation rather than assume that similar words share referents.

The strongest comparative warning is that immediate translation may manufacture the common object it claims to discover.

Coverage limits are substantial. The sample spans Yoruba, Māori/decolonial, Canadian and Australian Indigenous-methods discourse, Native North American technoscience, Andean worlds, and Amazonian perspectivism. It does not license claims about an “Indigenous worldview,” Africa as a whole, or non-Western epistemology generally. Asian, Islamic, South Asian, Pacific Islander beyond Māori, and many African traditions remain unsearched.

## Disagreements and countertraditions

The corpus contains no consensus on:

- **Bearer:** individual, group, institution, tradition, practice, or human-artifact system.
- **Norm:** truth, justification, reliability, successful performance, intelligibility, relational accountability, or communal authorization.
- **Mechanism:** interpretation, participation, testimony, criticism, inscription, classification, translation, or political struggle.
- **Relation to the world:** discovery, construction, articulation under resistance, enactment, or partial connection.
- **Change:** accumulation, apprenticeship, horizon transformation, paradigm rupture, infrastructural migration, or decolonial refusal.
- **Persistence:** memory in persons, collective reconstruction, archive, embodied repertoire, standards, or material routines.
- **Failure:** falsity, unreliability, exclusion, silencing, loss, mistranslation, incommensurability, or organized ignorance.
- **Comparability:** universal rational appraisal, local validity criteria, controlled equivocation, or irreducible ontological difference.

The classic rationality debate remains open-ended rather than dividing cleanly into universalism and “anything goes.” Hollis and Lukes’s collection explicitly ranges between objective universals and relativisms challenging a single world: Martin Hollis and Steven Lukes, eds., *Rationality and Relativism* (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1982), ISBN 9780262580618. [Publisher record](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262580618/rationality-and-relativism/).

## Concepts resisting unification or direct translation

- Gadamerian *Wirkungsgeschichte* is not equivalent to a causal history of belief.
- “Fusion of horizons” is not consensus aggregation.
- Fleck’s thought style is not simply Kuhn’s paradigm.
- Situated participation is not information transfer.
- Distributed cognition is not collective belief.
- Epistemic culture is not a worldview.
- Boundary objects enable cooperation without unified meaning.
- Archive and repertoire preserve differently.
- Testimonial knowledge, group knowledge, and institutional certification have different attribution conditions.
- Relational accountability is not an extra ethical constraint appended to otherwise unchanged Western epistemology.
- Controlled equivocation is not failed literal translation.
- Hermeneutical injustice is not merely missing vocabulary.
- Ignorance can be actively maintained rather than a zero quantity of knowledge.
- Indigenous political belonging cannot be translated into genetic information without changing the object at issue.

## Strongest likely cross-domain conflict

The strongest claim likely to conflict with an individual-cognitive, computational, or formal domain is:

> In many central cases, knowledge does not first form inside an individual and subsequently become social; the relevant epistemic capacity is constituted by historically inherited language, organized participation, testimony, artifacts, classifications, authority relations, and infrastructures.

The conflict arises because individualist or computational models can treat those factors as external inputs, channels, or implementation details. This corpus supplies cases where removing them changes the identity of the achievement and sometimes eliminates any determinate individual bearer. Hutchins’s navigation system, Hardwig’s epistemic dependence, Knorr Cetina’s epistemic cultures, Taylor’s repertoire, and Wilson’s relational accountability are especially resistant to internalist reconstruction.

This does not entail that truth, evidence, perception, or individual cognition are dispensable. It entails that they are insufficient as a general account of how socially attributable knowledge arises and persists.

## Evidence that would reverse or materially weaken this position

I would reverse the anti-unification conclusion if comparative evidence established all of the following:

1. a single operationally specified process predicts epistemic change across interpretive, scientific, embodied, Indigenous, institutional, and distributed cases;
2. the same bearer criterion identifies who or what knows without ad hoc redefinition;
3. the process distinguishes knowledge from error and organized ignorance across traditions without importing one tradition’s validation regime;
4. removing social-material organization leaves the relevant achievement intact rather than merely degraded;
5. translations preserve inferential, practical, ontological, and normative roles—not just lexical similarity;
6. longitudinal evidence shows one invariant sequence rather than several mechanisms connected only by family resemblance.

More locally, Gadamer’s position would be weakened by evidence that interpreters can reliably identify and suspend historically inherited prejudgments from an external methodical standpoint. Distributed accounts would be weakened if all system-level performance could be predictively and explanatorily reduced to individual states without loss. Power-centered accounts would be weakened if credibility, classification, and institutional access proved causally irrelevant after strong controls.

## Rejected synthesis move

Rejected: a universal sequence such as

> data → information → understanding → justified knowledge → collective memory.

It fails because:

- Gadamer denies a neutral “data” stage prior to interpretation;
- Lave and Wenger explain learning as participation rather than content conversion;
- Fleck and STS show that data and facts require prior styles, instruments, and practices;
- Hutchins distributes the process across a system;
- boundary objects coordinate without full semantic convergence;
- archive and repertoire preserve through different media;
- Fricker and Dotson show that a warranted contribution may fail to become socially recognized knowledge;
- Indigenous and comparative work shows that extracting detachable informational content may destroy relational, political, or ontological conditions of knowing.

The defensible synthesis is therefore not a common sequence but a controlled comparison of differently constituted epistemic achievements.

**Phase-A verdict:** “Knowledge formation” is usable as a question-index, provided every use identifies the epistemic achievement, bearer, validation/authority regime, social-material practices, and comparative translation limits. It is not supported as a unified formation process.
