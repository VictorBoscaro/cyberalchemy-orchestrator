## Native problem map

### 1. There is no primitive epistemic given

The 1868 cognition series denies four alleged capacities: immediate introspection, intuition as an unmediated cognition, thought without signs, and a conception of the absolutely incognizable. Cognition arises from prior cognition and is inferentially/signfully mediated; even self-knowledge is reconstructed from outward facts rather than transparently possessed. Peirce explicitly distinguishes the private self from a transcendental ego and treats a child’s self-consciousness as developmentally acquired through experience and testimony, not original. See Peirce, “Questions Concerning Certain Faculties Claimed for Man,” *Journal of Speculative Philosophy* 2 (1868): 103–114, [stable text](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p26.html), and “Some Consequences of Four Incapacities,” 2 (1868): 140–157, [stable text](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p27.html).

Problem generated: if every cognition is determined by prior cognitions, how does constraint from the object enter without either an intuition or a vicious regress?

### 2. Cognition is not merely in signs; cognition and selfhood develop as sign-processes

In the early account, every thought-sign is interpreted by a subsequent thought. Meaning is therefore virtual and prospective, not exhausted by a present mental episode. Peirce’s provocative consequence is that the “man-sign” and external symbols reciprocally educate one another: increases in a person’s information and in a word’s information are interdependent. This makes attribution to a self possible only through diachronic consistency and public sign relations, not through punctual possession.

Daniel Campos emphasizes that Peirce gives the sign logical priority over mind and builds his semeiotic model of mind from the 1868 denial of introspection. Daniel G. Campos, “Peirce’s Semeiotic Model of the Mind,” in Cheryl Misak, ed., *The Cambridge Companion to Peirce* (Cambridge University Press, 2004), pp. 238–264, [publisher record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-peirce/peirces-semeiotic-model-of-the-mind/D55D8B13C009203A9B06792E8385B64A).

Problem generated: “who knows?” cannot be answered simply by naming the organism in which a representation occurs.

### 3. Inquiry begins with disruption, not with a universal developmental origin

In “The Fixation of Belief,” genuine doubt irritates and inhibits an already established habit of action. Inquiry is the struggle that moves from doubt to settled belief. Belief persists as habit: it guides desire and action until surprise, recalcitrant experience, or conflicting beliefs unsettle it. Peirce describes four methods—tenacity, authority, a priori agreement, and science. All can settle opinion; only science deliberately subjects belief to an “external permanency” and public correction. Charles S. Peirce, “The Fixation of Belief,” *Popular Science Monthly* 12 (November 1877): 1–15, [stable text](https://peirce.org/writings/p107.html).

This is not a universal sequence from ignorance to knowledge. It presupposes prior habits, and many disturbances fail to become inquiry at all. Tenacity can suppress counterevidence; authority can remove exposure to alternatives; fashionable reason can make social congeniality look like rationality.

### 4. Persistence is habitual, social, and potentially pathological

Beliefs endure because they are rules of action embodied in dispositions. Institutions can stabilize them by controlling attention, teaching, expression, and contact. Hence social persistence is epistemically ambivalent: the same sociality that enables correction can enforce error. “Community” is not automatically epistemically virtuous; Peirce’s method of authority is itself communal belief-fixation.

This defeats the common shorthand “Peirce says knowledge is socially constructed by consensus.” A definite community’s agreement can be coercively wrong. Peirce’s real is independent of what any particular person or finite collection thinks.

### 5. The scientific method changes inquiry by making error avowable and correction structurally possible

Peirce’s stronger claim is methodological, not psychological: science is distinctive because its conception of its object and procedures allows present opinion to be wrong and experience to bear against it. Self-correction is not automatic, quick, or guaranteed for every actual research community. It is a property of indefinitely continued practices—replication, prediction, experiment, criticism, and revision—under suitable conditions.

The “First Rule of Logic” adds an ethical-affective condition: in order to learn, one must desire to learn and avoid blocking inquiry. The Indiana Peirce Edition Project’s editorial headnote identifies the contrast between Peirce’s “Will to Learn” and James’s “Will to Believe.” See Peirce Edition Project, headnotes to *The Essential Peirce*, vol. 2, [editorial record](https://peirce.sitehost.iu.edu/ep/ep2/headers/ep2headx.htm).

### 6. Discovery, consequence, and testing are irreducible functions

Peirce’s mature inquiry is not a single inference or generic update:

- Abduction/retroduction forms an explanatory hypothesis.
- Deduction derives experiential consequences.
- Induction tests those consequences and estimates the hypothesis’s standing.

The semantics changed. Early “hypothesis” was modeled as a permutation of rule, case, and result and functionally overlapped with induction. Later abduction became the only inferential operation that introduces a new idea, while induction acquired the distinct testing role. The late account is methodological and normative, not merely syllogistic.

Douglas Walton distinguishes this from modern inference to the best explanation and shows why collapsing them loses Peirce’s staged methodology. Douglas Walton, “Peirce Knew Why Abduction Isn’t IBE—A Scheme and Critical Questions for Abductive Argument,” *Argumentation* 32 (2018): 569–587, [doi:10.1007/s10503-017-9443-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10503-017-9443-9). See also Tomis Kapitan, “Peirce and the Autonomy of Abductive Reasoning,” *Erkenntnis* 37 (1992): 1–26, and the historical problem survey in Daniel J. McAuliffe, “Four Problems of Abduction: A Brief History,” *HOPOS* 1.2 (2011): 227–253, [doi:10.1086/660746](https://doi.org/10.1086/660746).

### 7. Perception does not provide an uncontaminated foundation, yet experience constrains interpretation

Because perceptual judgment is inferential, perception is continuous with hypothesis at one level. But mature semeiotic distinguishes the immediate object available in a sign from the dynamical object that constrains signification. “Collateral experience” supplies familiarity with the object that a sign alone cannot furnish. This prevents both foundationalist givenness and a frictionless “everything is interpretation” thesis.

Mats Bergman argues that collateral experience marks a limit of semiosis while also entering symbolic interpretation through contextual indication. Mats Bergman, “C. S. Peirce on Interpretation and Collateral Experience,” *Signs: International Journal of Semiotics* 4 (2010): 134–161, [stable article](https://tidsskrift.dk/signs/article/view/26855).

### 8. Meaning-growth is not identical with knowledge-growth

A sign determines an interpretant, often a more developed sign, but interpretants may be emotional, energetic, or logical; they may clarify, mislead, redirect, or merely reproduce a habit. Not every semiotic effect is informational, not every increase in information is knowledge, and deduction can transform explicitness without increasing empirical information. Albert Atkin’s historical reconstruction also notes disagreement over whether genuinely infinite semiosis survives into Peirce’s mature account. Albert Atkin, “Peirce’s Theory of Signs,” *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, 2022 archive, [entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2022/entries/peirce-semiotics/).

### 9. Truth is not actual consensus, and “the end of inquiry” is disputed

The 1878 formulation links truth with the opinion ultimately fated to be agreed upon and reality with its object. Yet finite agreement cannot constitute truth. Scholarship divides over whether Peirce offers:

- a literal convergence theory;
- a counterfactual or regulative account of what sufficiently persistent inquiry would settle;
- an account of assertion’s commitments;
- a realist correspondence account pragmatically clarified by inquiry rather than reduced to it.

“Buried secrets”—truths no future inquiry could recover—pressure literal convergence. Hookway argues that Peirce’s clarification can instead articulate commitments incurred in assertion without deciding realism versus anti-realism. Christopher Hookway, “Truth and the Convergence of Opinion,” in *Truth, Rationality, and Pragmatism: Themes from Peirce* (Oxford University Press, 2002), pp. 44–81, [doi:10.1093/0199256586.003.0003](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199256586.003.0003). See also Cheryl Misak, “Truth, Reality, and Convergence,” in *The Cambridge Companion to Peirce* (2004), pp. 127–149, [doi:10.1017/CCOL0521570069.005](https://doi.org/10.1017/CCOL0521570069.005).

### 10. Belief, confidence, and scientific hope are not stable synonyms

The neat 1877 doubt → inquiry → belief model is complicated by the 1898 Cambridge lectures, where Peirce says belief has no place in pure science. Scientific investigators should not act as if current theories were settled action-guiding commitments; they need confidence or hope sufficient to continue testing. In matters of vital practical importance, by contrast, action cannot await indefinite inquiry and must lean on evolved sentiment and instinct.

This is a genuine semantic and doctrinal tension, not a footnote. Christopher Hookway, “Belief, Confidence, and the Method of Science,” in *Truth, Rationality, and Pragmatism*, pp. 21–43, [doi:10.1093/0199256586.003.0002](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199256586.003.0002). The primary lecture is Peirce, “Philosophy and the Conduct of Life” (1898), in Peirce Edition Project, ed., *The Essential Peirce*, vol. 2 (Indiana University Press, 1998), pp. 27–41, [JSTOR stable item](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt16gz4vr.9).

Jeff Kasser further argues that Peirce’s probability writings allow confidence and evidential weight to weaken even when “belief,” strictly construed, has not yet been displaced. Jeff Kasser, “Genuine Belief and Genuine Doubt in Peirce,” *European Journal of Philosophy* 26.4 (2018): 1460–1481, [doi:10.1111/ejop.12360](https://doi.org/10.1111/ejop.12360).

### 11. Critical common-sensism supplies starting commitments without making them incorrigible

Peirce’s later “critical common-sensism” holds that inquiry must begin from beliefs not genuinely doubted in current practice. These are vague, inherited, affectively entrenched, and often indispensable. They are not Cartesian certainties: changing experience and scientific practice can eventually make them dubitable. This position modifies Thomas Reid while resisting both total skepticism and foundational infallibility.

Christopher Hookway, “Common Sense, Pragmatism, and Rationality,” in *Truth, Rationality, and Pragmatism*, pp. 198–222, [doi:10.1093/0199256586.003.0009](https://doi.org/10.1093/0199256586.003.0009). Primary locus: Peirce, “Issues of Pragmaticism,” *The Monist* 15.4 (1905): 481–499, catalogued with the related texts at the [Peirce Gateway](https://cspeirce.com/menu/library/bycsp/bycsp.htm).

### 12. Attribution remains distributed across person, signs, practices, and an indefinite community

A person can be the practical bearer of a habit and can assert, doubt, or test. But no isolated person is the ultimate epistemic subject. Thought depends on external signs and future interpretants; reality and truth implicate an indefinite community capable of increasing knowledge. Nevertheless, it is equally misleading to attribute knowledge to “the community” as a present group mind. Peirce’s community is normative, temporally open, and constrained by an independent object.

## Established positions and owners

- Anti-intuitionism, inferential self-knowledge, and thought in signs: Peirce, 1868 cognition series.
- Belief as action-guiding habit and inquiry as doubt-resolution: Peirce, 1877–78 *Illustrations of the Logic of Science*.
- Pragmatic clarification through conceivable practical bearings: Peirce, “How to Make Our Ideas Clear,” *Popular Science Monthly* 12 (January 1878): 286–302, [stable text](https://peirce.org/writings/p119.html).
- Truth/reality related to indefinitely continued inquiry but independent of finite opinion: Peirce; reconstructed differently by Cheryl Misak and Christopher Hookway.
- Triadic semiosis and interpretant-development: Peirce; major reconstructions by Savan, Liszka, T. L. Short, Atkin, and Campos.
- Collateral experience as a boundary condition on interpretation: mature Peirce; emphasized by Mats Bergman.
- Abduction–deduction–induction as differentiated inquiry functions: mature Peirce; historical clarification by Burks, Frankfurt, Fann, Anderson, McAuliffe, and Walton.
- Critical common-sensism and rational self-control: mature Peirce; developed by Hookway.
- Scientific confidence/hope versus belief and vital commitment: 1898 Peirce; reconstructed by Hookway and Kasser.

## Semantic changes that must be preserved

- “Intuition” in 1868 means a cognition not determined by a previous cognition of the same object, not simply an intelligent hunch.
- “Hypothesis” changes from an early syllogistic inference form toward “abduction,” “retroduction,” or “presumption” as the conjectural stage of inquiry.
- “Belief” changes from the 1877 terminus of inquiry to something Peirce sometimes excludes from pure science in 1898.
- “Community” changes function across texts: actual mutual influence, coercive authority, indefinite community of investigators, and ideal future reference point are not interchangeable.
- “Interpretant” is not simply a human interpretation; it can be another sign, an effect, a habit-change, or a normatively specified outcome.
- “Information” can grow in persons and words without thereby becoming warranted knowledge.
- “Pragmatism” becomes “pragmaticism” partly to separate Peirce’s logical doctrine from Jamesian and Schillerian appropriations.

## Disagreements and countertraditions

1. **Cartesian foundationalism:** Peirce denies immediate self-certifying intuition and universal methodological doubt. Counter-reading: André de Tienne and later scholars question whether Peirce’s anti-intuitionism is as anti-Cartesian as standard pragmatist historiography assumes. Rosa Maria Calcaterra et al., “Why Peirce’s Anti-Intuitionism Is Not Anti-Cartesian,” *International Journal of Philosophical Studies* 24.4 (2016): 562–581, [doi:10.1080/09672559.2016.1203981](https://doi.org/10.1080/09672559.2016.1203981).

2. **James’s will to believe:** Peirce’s “will to learn” privileges openness and indefinite correction; James permits commitment under forced, live, momentous options. Yet Peirce’s treatment of vital matters partly approaches James by assigning authority to instinct and sentiment.

3. **Consensus interpretations:** Peirce is frequently read as defining truth by community agreement. Realist and assertion-based readings reject this: convergence, if retained, is constrained by the object and cannot mean actual majority assent.

4. **Infinite semiosis:** some interpreters take endless interpretant chains as constitutive; Short argues that this is characteristic mainly of the early theory, while mature final interpretants and habits provide closure of a qualified kind.

5. **Abduction as IBE:** much later literature equates them. Historical scholarship rejects the equation because Peircean abduction licenses a testable conjecture but does not by itself establish that conjecture as the best or probably true explanation.

6. **Psychologism:** the irritation-of-doubt model sounds psychological; Peirce’s later normative sciences make logic a theory of how thought ought to be self-controlled. Kasser’s belief/confidence distinction moderates but does not erase the tension.

7. **Ubiquitous semiosis versus experiential resistance:** maximal semiotic readings risk absorbing the object into interpretation. Collateral experience and Secondness provide resistance not generated by signs alone.

8. **Individualism versus strong group mind:** Peirce rejects epistemic atomism, but his texts do not straightforwardly license contemporary metaphysical group knowledge. The indefinite community is a regulative and temporal structure, not merely a collective agent.

## Concepts resisting unification

These should not be merged into one process variable:

- Cognition, belief, knowledge, confidence, hope, opinion, habit, information, interpretant, and truth.
- Individual habit and communal settlement.
- Actual consensus and ideal-limit convergence.
- Emotional, energetic, and logical interpretants.
- Abductive novelty, deductive explication, and inductive testing.
- Perceptual judgment and collateral experience.
- Common-sense indubitability and certainty.
- Scientific inquiry and decisions in vital matters.
- The cessation of doubt and attainment of truth.
- Semiotic development and epistemic warrant.
- Human learning and cosmological habit-taking.

## Is “knowledge formation” coherent in this corpus?

Only as an external umbrella, and only if explicitly disjunctive. Peirce does not supply a single formation process with a universal origin, fixed subject, or invariant endpoint. The phrase can coherently name a family of linked but non-identical transitions:

- prior cognition → further cognition;
- surprise/real doubt → inquiry;
- abduction → deduced consequences → inductive testing;
- interpretation → habit-change;
- private uncertainty → public criticism;
- present belief/confidence → future correction;
- inherited common sense → newly dubitable proposition.

It becomes incoherent if it implies that knowledge is a substance produced at a determinate moment, that every inquiry begins in ignorance, that settlement entails truth, or that one subject owns the outcome.

## Strongest cross-domain conflict expected

The strongest likely conflict is:

> Epistemic attribution cannot be made adequate by identifying an individual organism’s internal state at a time; for Peirce, cognitively significant content and warrant are diachronic, sign-mediated, environmentally constrained, and answerable to a temporally indefinite community.

This should conflict sharply with domains that model learning or knowing as an internal state transition of a bounded agent, especially individualist cognitive psychology, neuroscience, Bayesian-agent models, and some machine-learning accounts. The conflict is not that Peirce denies individual cognition; it is that individual state is insufficient to constitute the relevant meaning and authority.

## Evidence that would reverse or materially weaken this position

I would reverse the strong anti-individualist formulation if manuscript-level chronological study showed that:

- Peirce explicitly abandoned the 1868 “man-sign,” external-sign, or indefinite-community theses in his mature work;
- mature final interpretants were consistently private occurrent mental states rather than habits/norms;
- “community” functioned only as a contingent aid to individual warrant, with no constitutive role in truth, reality, or meaning;
- collateral experience supplied an immediately self-authenticating individual given;
- the 1898 belief/science distinction was merely rhetorical and had no systematic role in his normative logic.

I would weaken the broader map if “knowledge” proved to be a stable technical Peircean kind subsuming belief, information, inquiry, and interpretants across periods. The searched corpus instead supplies repeated counterexamples to that unification.

## Rejected synthesis move

Rejected: **“Peirce offers a universal cycle in which surprise causes abduction, deduction, induction, communal consensus, and therefore knowledge.”**

Why rejected:

- Not all doubt becomes inquiry.
- Not all inquiry follows a clean three-stage temporal sequence.
- Abduction proposes; it does not warrant truth by itself.
- Deduction need not increase information.
- Induction yields fallible standing, not finality.
- Settlement can be produced by tenacity or authority.
- Actual consensus can be false.
- Scientific inquiry may require hope or confidence rather than settled belief.
- Vital decisions obey a different practical economy.
- Semiosis is broader than knowledge, while collateral experience prevents semiosis from becoming a closed self-producing cycle.
