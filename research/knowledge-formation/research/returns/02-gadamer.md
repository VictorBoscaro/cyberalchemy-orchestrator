# Gadamer / social-comparative return

## Phase A — frozen external map

This section faithfully compresses the completed repository-blind phase-A report. Its claims are frozen; phase B does not revise them.

### Verdict

“Knowledge formation” is coherent only as a provisional comparative heading, not as a common native object, natural kind, or universal sequence. The surveyed traditions concern at least: interpretive understanding; historically conditioned intelligibility; entry into competent practice; stabilization and warrant of facts; distributed cognition; testimonial and institutional dependence; preservation and forgetting; attribution and credit; and organized ignorance, unintelligibility, or silence.

A responsible comparison indexes:

`<epistemic achievement, bearer, practice/media, warrant-or-authority regime, temporal-material supports>`.

Without those indices, “formation” equivocates among understanding, learning, belief acquisition, skill, fact stabilization, certification, memory, recognition, and attribution.

Inclusion: primary theory altering the bearer, production, warrant, transmission, or persistence of knowledge; ethnographic mechanisms; primary counterpositions defending truth or universal appraisal; authoritative reference works; stable publisher/journal/DOI/library records.

Exclusion: generic knowledge-management and DIKW pipelines; machine-learning uses except where directly relevant to testimony/distribution; cognitive-developmental and animal social-learning corpora; popular summaries; theology; most legal epistemology; and unsupported generalizations about “Asian,” “African,” or “Indigenous” epistemology. These are coverage limits, not irrelevance verdicts.

Stopping rationale: conceptual saturation was reached around the recurring axes individual/system, discovery/construction, truth/authorization, archive/repertoire, translation/equivocation, and understanding/ideology. Later searches added metadata rather than a new mechanism. This was a structured map, not a systematic review or corpus-closure claim.

### Established positions and owners

1. **Understanding as event.** Gadamer's *Verstehen* occurs within effective history. Prejudgments enable and constrain intelligibility; application belongs to understanding; a fusion of horizons changes interpreter and matter rather than depositing content in an unchanged subject. Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method* (1960; revised English trans. Joel Weinsheimer and Donald G. Marshall, Bloomsbury/Continuum, 2004/2013), https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/truth-and-method-9781780936246/. Habermas's counterposition: tradition-mediated agreement can reproduce domination and systematic distortion; dialogical openness alone may not distinguish truth from ideology. SEP: https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2022/entries/hermeneutics/.

2. **Social location.** Karl Mannheim, *Ideology and Utopia* (1929/1936), https://www.routledge.com/Ideology-and-Utopia/Mannheim/p/book/9781315002828, treats thought as socially and historically located, creating the reflexive problem that sociology of knowledge is located too. Sandra Harding, *Whose Science? Whose Knowledge?* (Cornell UP, 1991), ISBN 9780801425134, https://www.cornellpress.cornell.edu/book/9780801425134/whose-science-whose-knowledge/, makes marginalized standpoint a critical achievement, not an automatic possession of identity.

3. **Collective stabilization of facts.** Ludwik Fleck, *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact* (1935; Chicago UP ed. 1979/1981), ISBN 9780226253251, https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/G/bo25676016.html: facts develop in thought collectives/styles under historical conditioning and worldly resistance. Thomas Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962; 4th ed. Chicago UP, 2012), https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo13179781.html: normal science, anomaly, crisis, and paradigm reorganization replace monotonic accumulation. David Bloor, *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976; 2nd ed. Chicago UP, 1991), https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/K/bo3684600.html: explain accepted and rejected beliefs symmetrically and reflexively.

4. **Construction counterpositions.** Ian Hacking, *The Social Construction of What?* (Harvard UP, 1999), https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Social_Construction_of_What.html?id=C3ILEAAAQBAJ, separates constructions of classifications, people, ideas, objects, and facts. Paul Boghossian, *Fear of Knowledge* (OUP, 2006), DOI https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199287185.001.0001, rejects strong constructivism and relativism. Bruno Latour, “Why Has Critique Run out of Steam?”, *Critical Inquiry* 30.2 (2004): 225–248, DOI https://doi.org/10.1086/421123, warns that indiscriminate debunking can empower denial and redirects attention to matters of concern. “Socially made” does not decide truth, warrant, imposition, robustness, or fragility.

5. **Competent participation.** Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger, *Situated Learning* (Cambridge UP, 1991), DOI https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355: learning changes participation, identity, access, and skilled practice through legitimate peripheral participation. Exclusion from legitimate participation is a failure mechanism. The model does not cover every guarded, secret, complementary, or rupture-driven practice.

6. **Distributed bearer.** Edwin Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (MIT Press, 1995/1996), ISBN 9780262581462, https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262581462/cognition-in-the-wild/: teams, artifacts, procedures, and environments can compose a cognitive system. Ronald Giere and Barton Moffatt, “Distributed Cognition,” *Social Studies of Science* 33.2 (2003): 301–310, DOI https://doi.org/10.1177/03063127030332017, dissolves a simple cognitive/social boundary. Individual, team, institution, and coupled system remain rival bearers.

7. **Dependence and criticism.** John Hardwig, “Epistemic Dependence,” *Journal of Philosophy* 82.7 (1985): 335–349, DOI https://doi.org/10.2307/2026523: advanced knowledge can depend on evidence no individual possesses. Alvin Goldman, *Knowledge in a Social World* (OUP, 1999), ISBN 9780191519284, https://books.google.com/books/about/Knowledge_in_a_Social_World.html?id=lQdREAAAQBAJ: assess social practices by truth-conducive outputs, not consensus alone. Helen Longino, *Science as Social Knowledge* (Princeton UP, 1990) and *The Fate of Knowledge* (2002): objectivity is achieved through organized criticism, uptake, shared standards, and tempered equality of authority. Sociality may transmit, generate, distribute, or constitute warrant; those theses differ.

8. **Epistemic cultures and cooperation without consensus.** Karin Knorr Cetina, *Epistemic Cultures* (Harvard UP, 1999), DOI https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674039681: high-energy physics and molecular biology use distinct object, instrument, empirical, and warranting arrangements. Susan Leigh Star and James Griesemer, “Institutional Ecology, ‘Translations’ and Boundary Objects,” *Social Studies of Science* 19.3 (1989): 387–420, DOI https://doi.org/10.1177/030631289019003001: heterogeneous communities coordinate through locally adaptable yet cross-site stable objects. Cooperation need not culminate in shared meaning or fusion.

9. **Classification, memory, archive, repertoire.** Geoffrey Bowker and Susan Leigh Star, *Sorting Things Out* (MIT Press, 1999), ISBN 9780262024617, https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262024617/sorting-things-out/: standards make entities visible/actionable while producing residues and exclusions. Geoffrey Bowker, *Memory Practices in the Sciences* (MIT Press, 2005/2006), ISBN 9780262025898, https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262025898/memory-practices-in-the-sciences/: recording infrastructures shape scientific memory and forgetting. Maurice Halbwachs, *On Collective Memory*, ed./trans. Lewis Coser (Chicago UP, 1992), ISBN 9780226115962, https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/O/bo3619875.html: groups reconstruct rather than merely retrieve the past. Diana Taylor, *The Archive and the Repertoire* (Duke UP, 2003), https://www.dukeupress.edu/the-archive-and-the-repertoire: gesture, movement, speech, and performance store and transmit differently from written archives.

10. **Attribution and failure.** Robert Merton, “The Matthew Effect in Science,” *Science* 159.3810 (1968): 56–63, DOI https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56: prestige and cumulative advantage distort credit allocation. Miranda Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice* (OUP, 2007), DOI https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001: testimonial injustice produces credibility deficits; hermeneutical injustice reflects unequal participation in shared interpretive resources. Kristie Dotson, “Tracking Epistemic Violence,” *Hypatia* 26.2 (2011): 236–257, DOI https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1527-2001.2011.01177.x: pernicious ignorance can make hearers fail speakers' vulnerabilities. Shannon Sullivan and Nancy Tuana, eds., *Race and Epistemologies of Ignorance* (SUNY Press, 2007), ISBN 9780791471029, https://sunypress.edu/Books/R/Race-and-Epistemologies-of-Ignorance: ignorance can be socially produced and maintained.

### Comparative limits and translation-resistant concepts

Fredrik Barth, “An Anthropology of Knowledge,” *Current Anthropology* 43.1 (2002): 1–18, DOI https://doi.org/10.1086/324131, compares substantive corpus, media of representation, and social organization. Even this heuristic may over-unify knowing that cannot be detached from doing, being, land, or obligation.

Helen Verran, *Science and an African Logic* (Chicago UP, 2001), ISBN 9780226853895, https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo3631540.html, shows Yoruba numbering and certainty enacted through distinct social-material practices. Linda Tuhiwai Smith, *Decolonizing Methodologies*, 3rd ed. (Zed/Bloomsbury, 2021; orig. 1999), ISBN 9781350346086, https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/decolonizing-methodologies-9781350346086/, analyzes research as an imperial regime of extraction and authorization. Shawn Wilson, *Research Is Ceremony* (Fernwood, 2008), ISBN 9781552662816, https://cup.columbia.edu/book/research-is-ceremony/9781552662816/, joins ontology, epistemology, methodology, and axiology through relational accountability. Kim TallBear, *Native American DNA* (Minnesota UP, 2013), DOI https://doi.org/10.5749/minnesota/9780816665853.001.0001, shows that genetic evidence does not determine political belonging. Marisol de la Cadena, *Earth Beings* (Duke UP, 2015), DOI https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822375265, uses partial connections without reducing earth-beings to cultural belief about universal nature. Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, “Perspectival Anthropology and the Method of Controlled Equivocation,” *Tipití* 2.1 (2004), DOI https://doi.org/10.70845/2572-3626.1010, preserves equivocation rather than assuming shared referents.

Coverage does not license an “Indigenous worldview,” an Africa-wide claim, or a general “non-Western epistemology.” Asian, Islamic, South Asian, much Pacific and African scholarship remained outside the search.

Concepts resisting unification: *Wirkungsgeschichte* versus causal belief history; fusion versus consensus; thought style versus paradigm; participation versus information transfer; distributed cognition versus collective belief; epistemic culture versus worldview; boundary-object coordination versus semantic unity; archive versus repertoire; testimony versus group knowledge versus certification; relational accountability versus an added ethics constraint; controlled equivocation versus failed translation; hermeneutical injustice versus missing vocabulary; produced ignorance versus absent knowledge; political belonging versus genetic information.

### Principal disagreements

- Bearer: individual, group, institution, tradition, practice, or coupled system.
- Norm: truth, justification, reliability, competent performance, intelligibility, relational accountability, or authorization.
- Mechanism: interpretation, participation, testimony, criticism, inscription, classification, translation, or struggle.
- World relation: discovery, construction, resistance-conditioned articulation, enactment, or partial connection.
- Change: accumulation, apprenticeship, horizon transformation, paradigm rupture, migration, or refusal.
- Persistence: personal memory, collective reconstruction, archive, embodied repertoire, standards, or routine.
- Failure: falsity, unreliability, exclusion, silence, loss, mistranslation, incommensurability, or organized ignorance.
- Comparison: universal appraisal, local criteria, controlled equivocation, or ontological difference.

Martin Hollis and Steven Lukes, eds., *Rationality and Relativism* (MIT Press, 1982), ISBN 9780262580618, https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262580618/rationality-and-relativism/, demonstrates that the rationality debate yields multiple positions rather than “universalism versus anything goes.”

### Strongest expected cross-domain conflict

In many central cases, knowledge does not first form inside an individual and later become social. Historically inherited language, participation, testimony, artifacts, classifications, authority, and infrastructures partly constitute the epistemic capacity. This conflicts with individual-cognitive, computational, or formal models that treat those conditions as external inputs or implementation detail. It does not make truth, evidence, perception, or individual cognition dispensable; it makes them insufficient as a general account.

### Reversal evidence

The anti-unification position should be reversed if evidence establishes a single operational process that: predicts change across interpretive, scientific, embodied, Indigenous, institutional, and distributed cases; identifies one bearer criterion without ad hoc redefinition; distinguishes knowledge from error and organized ignorance without importing one tradition's validation regime; survives removal of social-material organization; preserves inferential, practical, ontological, and normative roles in translation; and displays an invariant longitudinal sequence.

Locally, Gadamer would be weakened if interpreters reliably suspended historically inherited prejudgments from an external methodical standpoint; distributed accounts if system performance reduced to individual states without explanatory loss; power accounts if credibility, classification, and access were causally irrelevant under strong controls.

### Rejected synthesis move

Rejected: `data → information → understanding → justified knowledge → collective memory`.

It assumes neutral data before interpretation; treats practice as content conversion; ignores instruments and thought styles; presumes an individual bearer; mistakes coordination for semantic convergence; collapses archive into repertoire; overlooks warranted contributions denied recognition; and can destroy relational, political, or ontological conditions by extracting detachable information.

The acceptable synthesis is a controlled comparison of differently constituted epistemic achievements, not a common sequence.

## Phase B — boundary comparison

Boundary read: `research/knowledge-formation/research-initial-definitions.md` only. No other internal project artifact was consulted. No supplemental external search was required because every comparison below is directly tested by sources already frozen in phase A.

### Confirmations

1. **No universal origin or question-first beginning.** This agrees with Gadamer's absence of a pre-interpretive start and with practice/distribution accounts in which inquiry need not begin as an explicit question.
2. **Narrative order is not ontological order.** This directly blocks the rejected pipeline.
3. **Non-equivalence of information, learning, understanding, belief, memory, skill, testimony, justification, and knowledge.** This matches the frozen map's central distinction.
4. **No isolated universal subject.** Individual, relational, practical, social, institutional, and distributed possibilities align with Hutchins, Hardwig, Lave/Wenger, and Knorr Cetina.
5. **Expository entry is not an origin claim.** This is a precise and necessary safeguard.
6. **Statement preservation may lose understanding conditions.** Bowker, Taylor, Halbwachs, and practice theory strongly confirm this.
7. **Internal vocabulary is not privileged over scholarship.** This is methodologically sound if evidence types remain distinguished.
8. **The operational baseline is explicitly demoted.** Calling “understanding accepted under recorded conditions” an architectural proposal rather than an account of formation correctly limits its authority.

### Imposed or potentially loading vocabulary

- **“Knowledge acquiring form.”** This metaphor suggests a prior formless substrate and a directed result. Some traditions concern changed participation, stabilized facts, disclosure, recognition, or maintained relations—not form acquisition.
- **“Movements.”** Useful phenomenologically, but it may smuggle temporal continuity across discontinuous paradigm rupture, attribution events, or infrastructural persistence.
- **“Phenomenological introduction.”** This assigns one tradition editorial priority. It must not become the classifier through which social epistemology, STS, or Indigenous accounts are redescribed.
- **“Conditions.”** In the baseline it gathers provenance, evidence, review, scope, version, and state. These differ as warrant, accountability, administrative state, and reuse metadata.
- **“Internal and external evidence.”** Equal non-privilege is right, but the two are not the same evidence type: local definitions evidence project use; scholarship supports descriptive, historical, empirical, or normative claims.

### Omissions

1. **Power and failure:** organized ignorance, testimonial silencing, hermeneutical marginalization, and epistemic violence.
2. **Recognition and credit:** who caused, contributed to, knows, is authorized, and receives credit can diverge.
3. **Classification and standards:** infrastructures do not only preserve; they constitute visibility and produce residual cases.
4. **Embodied repertoire:** the gaps mention embodiment and skill but not performance as a knowledge-preserving medium distinct from archive.
5. **Cooperation without consensus:** boundary objects show that shared work need not require common understanding.
6. **Comparative translation:** equivocation, partial connection, and ontological difference are absent. Without them, “same investigation” may impose a common object.
7. **Truth/realism countertradition:** causal genesis, social acceptance, justification, and truth must remain separate questions.
8. **Relational accountability and refusal:** knowledge practices may carry obligations and limits on extraction, not just alternate content.
9. **Worldly resistance:** social conditioning does not entail unconstrained construction.

### False equivalences and category risks

- **“Subject or unit”** conflates at least bearer, cognitive site, contributor, object of attribution, certifying authority, beneficiary, and accountable party.
- **“Permanence” options** mix persistence mechanisms (memory, repeatable skill, record) with social statuses (acceptance) and functional outcomes (reliable orientation).
- **“Understanding accepted under recorded conditions”** would be false as a general epistemic equivalence. Acceptance is neither necessary nor sufficient for knowledge; recorded provenance supports governance but does not guarantee truth or understanding.
- **“Transmission”** should not be one operation. Statement copying, testimonial warrant, skill apprenticeship, embodied reenactment, translation, and institutional reproduction have different identity and loss conditions.
- **“Formation” and “learning”** cannot serve as mutual proxies. A practice or institution may gain an epistemic capacity while no person undergoes the corresponding learning, and a person may learn without producing knowledge.

### Corrections recommended

1. Replace the singular research object with **epistemic achievements and transformations sometimes called knowledge**.
2. Require each account to state: achievement, bearer/site, contributors, attribution target, practice/media, warrant/authority regime, temporal-material support, and failure mode.
3. Keep **formation, learning, stabilization, recognition, attribution, authorization, transmission, preservation, revision, and loss** distinct, while allowing co-constitution.
4. State explicitly that agreement and acceptance can stabilize error or domination; neither is an epistemic success criterion by itself.
5. Add a comparison safeguard: similar terms do not guarantee shared referents; record equivocation and untranslatable residue.
6. Add archive/repertoire and boundary-object cases so persistence and coordination are not modeled only through statements.
7. Add a power question: who can speak, be heard, classify, preserve, refuse, authorize, and receive credit?
8. Type the architectural baseline as **governed-reuse eligibility**, not knowledge simpliciter.
9. If the essay opens phenomenologically, label understanding as one situated entrance and introduce its collapse-test inline: if other traditions can only appear after translation into changes of understanding, the opening has become an imposed universal frame.

### Boundary-level conclusion

The initial definitions are unusually careful and are largely confirmed. They already prevent the most damaging universal-sequence and isolated-subject errors. Their remaining weakness is not overt conflation but an under-typed umbrella: “formation,” “movement,” “subject or unit,” “conditions,” “transmission,” and “permanence” still gather mechanisms that the external corpus keeps separate. The correction is a comparison grammar, not a replacement master narrative.
