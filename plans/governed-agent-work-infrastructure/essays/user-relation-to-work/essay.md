# The User's Relation to the Work

## 1. When work grows beyond direct attention

A person may begin with something they want to achieve without knowing all the work it will
require. At first, they can remain close to nearly every decision because the intention is broad
and the work is still small. As the objective gains shape, different parts begin to proceed on
their own. The work becomes more capable of advancing, but it no longer fits within a single field
of attention.

This is not only a consequence of scale. Even a modest effort can contain work that needs attention
at different times. One part may continue without intervention while another reaches a question
that changes what should happen next. Keeping both equally present would not make the person better
informed. It would make the work that requires judgment harder to distinguish from the work that
does not.

A system capable of carrying more work could therefore increase the user's burden instead of
reducing it. If every new development has to be followed directly, greater capacity creates another
kind of work: supervising the machinery that was meant to help. A person can spend more attention
maintaining awareness of the work than judging whether it still serves what they were trying to
achieve.

The problem is not simply how to show less. Work that disappears completely from view can drift
from its purpose or return with consequences the person can no longer explain. The problem is how
someone can remain close to the objective while the work develops beyond their direct attention,
and how what becomes relevant can return into view before it requires judgment or action. That
relation cannot remain at one fixed depth.
